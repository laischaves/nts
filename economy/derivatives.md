# Derivatives

Derivatives are contracts that depend on the values of one or more financial variables (underlying: [[ativo subjacente]]). They can be _linear_ or _non-linear_ products, which describes their behavior.

**Linear derivatives** provide a pay-off linearly related to the value of the underlying asset, e.g.: forward contracts.

**Non-linear derivatives** provide a pay-off that is a non-linear function of the value of the underlyings, e.g.: options.

Although the underlying variables are central to the calculation of both types of derivatives, there are multiple other factors that can be just as important, such as interest rates.

Derivatives can be traded on both types of markets, Exchange-Traded Markets and Over-the-Markets.

# Types of Derivatives

## Forward Contracts

A forward contract is an _over-the-counter_ contract -- i.e., an contract made and agreed upon by both parties, constructed as they wished, -- where one party agrees to buy an asset for a _predetermined price at a predetermined future time_ and the other party agrees to sell the asset for the that price at that future time.
A forward contract is also a _linear_ derivative, meaning their payoff is linearly related to the value of the underlying asset at maturity.

In these forward contracts -- as well as in other derivative contracts, -- the party buying has a _long forward position_ whereas the party selling has a _short forward position_. The asset price is called the _forward price_.

> It can be said that entering a **short** position is betting against the price of the asset, whilst entering a **long** position is betting in favor of it.

## Futures Contracts

Futures are similar to forwards, but they trade on exchanges instead of OTC. That means that futures contracts have defined sets of rules -- designed by the exchange they're being traded on, -- such as maturity dates and assets that can be traded.

These types of contracts are used on a wide range of underlying assets, like the prices of metals, energy products, agricultural products, real estate indices and etc.

> Forwards and futures can be used to lock in the price for a future transaction.
## Options

Options are non-linear derivatives that give the holder the **right, but not the obligation** to either _buy_ or _sell_ an asset at a predetermined price in the future. This type of contract has a price -- known as a **premium**, -- which grants you said right, to be paid by the outset.

There are 2 types of options contracts: *call* and _put_ options. 

- The **call option** gives the party with the _long position_ the right to _buy_ an asset from the party with the short position for a certain price (called strike or exercise price) at a future time. So in this case, the "buyer" pays a premium for a call option contract, so that they get the right to buy an asset at the strike price at maturity.

- The **put option** gives the party with the _long position_ the right to _sell_ an asset to the party with the short position for a certain price (the strike price) at a future time. In this case, the "seller" pays a premium for a put option contract, so that they get the right to sell an asset at the strike price at maturity.

> The value of an option is a _non-linear_ function of the value of the underlying and the value of an option is dependent on the _volatility_ of the underlying.

Options can be used to buy protection against unfavorable movements while allowing the holder to benefit from favorable movements.

# Derivatives Traders

## Hedgers

Hedgers use derivatives to offset risks or exposures, often trying to net them. E.g., they can use them to secure themselves on foreign exchange risks.

This type of trader is usually concerned with reducing risk more than profiting from market variance.
## Speculators

Derivatives can be used for speculation as well, as their upfront payments are relatively small when compared to other investment products.
## Arbitrageurs

Arbitrage involves taking advantage of inconsistent pricing across two or more markets.
